# A second, skeptical reading

Another reader has said that each later passage in your packet (from
Genesis–Esther) is tied to the earlier passage beside it, in the way its
**kind** says:

- `back`: the later passage **names or retells an earlier happening** the
  earlier passage tells, with a detail from that account.
- `fulfilled`: the later passage **says something happened as an earlier
  word said** (a curse, a prophecy, a promise), and the earlier passage is
  that word.
- `carried_out`: the later passage **does what an earlier instruction
  commanded** -- said in so many words ("as the LORD had commanded Moses")
  or in the same details.
- `quotes`: the later passage **quotes the earlier passage's words**.

Test that, independently. Read **every word of both passages** for every
item. All text is the Berean Standard Bible.

Try to break the claim:
- Is it the same happening, word or command, or only the same person,
  place or subject?
- Does the later passage carry a detail of the earlier account, or only a
  word both happen to use?
- For `fulfilled`: does the later passage itself say it happened as was
  said? A thing that happens to match an earlier word, with nothing in the
  text tying them, is not `fulfilled`.
- For `carried_out`: is it the same command, done -- not a similar act?
- Is it a parallel telling of the same event in another book (Chronicles
  beside Samuel–Kings)? That is not a link.

**One rule, stated plainly, because readers took it two ways:** two passages
in the **same book** can be linked. A later chapter recalling a distinct,
earlier happening in the same book is `back` (Genesis 26:1 recalls the
famine "in Abraham's time" of Genesis 12:10). What is not a link is a
passage that simply continues the scene before it, or a summary that
restates what was just told.

Decide:
- `clear`: unmistakable. Quote, **copied exactly**, the later passage's
  words that carry the tie (`later_words`, with `later_verse`) and the
  earlier passage's words they match (`earlier_words`, with
  `earlier_verse`). A machine checks both. If the kind is wrong, give the
  right one in `kind`.
- `thin`: possible, but it rests on a shared subject, a common phrase, or
  a match the text does not draw.
- `no`: not tied as claimed, or a continuation of the same scene.

Never say what anything means, why it was written, or that one passage
looks forward to another beyond what the later passage itself says.
