# Reading the letters for references to events

You are one of seven readers. Your packet is part of Romans–Jude in the Berean
Standard Bible, every verse numbered. Read **every verse** of it, in order.
Find every place where the letter refers to an **event**: something the Bible
narrates as happening, told in a narrative passage.

## What counts

An event is a happening told as narrative: Genesis–Esther, the Gospels, Acts,
and the narrative parts of other books (Daniel 1–6, Jonah, Job 1–2 and 42).
Old Testament and New Testament events both count: Cain and Abel; the flood;
the exodus; Balaam; Elijah and the drought; the Last Supper; the
transfiguration; Jesus before Pilate; the resurrection appearances; Paul's
escape from Damascus.

A letter refers to an event when it **names it or recounts it with at least
one concrete detail from the account**: who was there, where, when, or what
was done. Examples that count:

- Hebrews 11:4, "By faith Abel offered God a better sacrifice than Cain did"
  (Genesis 4:3–8)
- 1 Corinthians 10:1–2, "our forefathers were all under the cloud, and they
  all passed through the sea" (Exodus 13:21–22; 14:21–31)
- 1 Corinthians 11:23, "The Lord Jesus, on the night He was betrayed, took
  bread" (the Last Supper)
- 2 Corinthians 11:33, "I was lowered in a basket through a window in the
  wall" (Acts 9:23–25)
- 2 Peter 1:17–18, "we ourselves heard this voice ... when we were with Him on
  the holy mountain" (the transfiguration)

## What does not count

- Doctrine about an event with no detail from its account: "Christ died for
  the ungodly" (Romans 5:6), "He was raised on the third day" stated as a
  creed with no narrative detail. (A *named* detail — "under Pontius Pilate",
  "appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve" — does count.)
- A quotation of the Law, a psalm or a prophet used as Scripture, unless the
  letter uses it to point at a happening.
- Future events (the Lord's coming), and the letter's own present
  circumstances unless a narrative passage tells them.
- Figures and comparisons that borrow a word without pointing at the
  happening.

When unsure, include it and say so in `note`; the judges who follow decide.
Missing a reference is worse than listing a doubtful one.

## Neutrality

Name what the text names. Do not explain what an event means. Where readers
and traditions dispute **which** account a passage refers to (Galatians 2:1–10
and Acts 11 or Acts 15, for instance), list it with `"disputed": true` and
every candidate passage — do not choose.

## Output

Write one JSON file to the path you are given, exactly this shape:

```json
{
  "chapters_read": {"Romans 1": 0, "Romans 4": 3, "...": 0},
  "refs": [
    {
      "letter": "Hebrews 11:4",
      "letter_end": "Hebrews 11:4",
      "quote": "By faith Abel offered God a better sacrifice than Cain did",
      "event": "Cain and Abel's offerings",
      "passages": ["Genesis 4:3-8"],
      "listed_event": "Cain and Abel",
      "how": "names",
      "disputed": false,
      "note": ""
    }
  ]
}
```

- `chapters_read`: **every** chapter in your packet, with how many references
  you found in it (0 is a valid answer). A chapter missing from this map means
  you did not read it.
- `quote`: the letter's own words, **copied exactly** from the packet (a
  machine checks every one). Shortest span that shows the reference.
- `passages`: the narrative passage(s) that tell the event, as references.
- `listed_event`: the matching title from `events.txt` if one fits, else null.
- `how`: `names` (names the person/place and the act), `retells` (recounts
  it), `words` (takes up the account's own words), or `alludes` (an
  unmistakable detail without naming).
- One entry per reference. A verse that refers to two events is two entries.

When done, reply with one line: how many references, and how many chapters.
