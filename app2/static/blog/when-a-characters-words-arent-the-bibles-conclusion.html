When a Character’s Words Aren’t the Bible’s Conclusion

October 31, 2026

Paul quotes Eliphaz.

In 1 Corinthians 3:19 he writes, “For the wisdom of this world is foolishness in God’s sight. As it is written: ‘He catches the wise in their craftiness.’” That quotation comes from Job 5:13, where the line is spoken by Eliphaz the Temanite, the first of Job’s three friends.

But then thirty-seven chapters later, as the book concludes, God speaks to that same man: “My wrath is kindled against you and your two friends. For you have not spoken about Me accurately, as My servant Job has” (Job 42:7).

Both verses are in the Bible. One treats a sentence of Eliphaz’s as Scripture to be cited. The other says Eliphaz did not speak accurately about God. If you have ever said “the Bible says…” and then quoted a line from Job, this is worth sitting with for a minute.

Most of Job is someone talking

Job is often remembered as a story of suffering, but structurally it is dominated by speeches. After a brief introduction the book is almost entirely a sequence of dialogues and monologues, followed by a short conclusion.

The Berean Standard Bible divides Job into sections with headings, and the headings name who is speaking: “Eliphaz: The Innocent Prosper,” “Job Replies: My Complaint Is Just,” “Bildad: Job Should Repent.” Counting the verses under each heading gives the shape of the book:

These counts follow the speaker headings. Each “Then Eliphaz replied,” for example, is counted with the speech it introduces. Chapter 28, the poem about wisdom, is counted with Job because it sits within his speeches and the heading does not identify another speaker. The narrator’s verses are mainly in chapters 1 and 2 and the closing section in chapter 42.

So, roughly 96 out of every 100 verses in Job are someone speaking. And about a fifth of the book is made up of speeches from the three friends whom God later tells that they have not spoken about Him correctly.

That makes the identity of the speaker pretty important.

The problem this makes for a knowledge graph

Bible Ontology builds a graph from the text. It identifies people, places, events, and relationships, and connects them to the verses where they appear.

For example, Genesis 4:8 tells us that Cain rose up against his brother Abel and killed him. That can be represented as a relationship: Cain killed Abel. The verse is attached to the relationship so that someone looking at it can go back to the source.

Now apply that same approach to Job.

Eliphaz says that God “catches the wise in their craftiness.” Bildad speaks about God punishing the wicked. Zophar speaks about the destruction that awaits the wicked. These are all statements in the book, and they can all be turned into relationships in a graph.

But if I extract those statements in the same way I extract the statement about Cain and Abel, I have a problem. The graph could end up presenting Eliphaz’s words as though they were simply facts being stated by the Bible. It could do the same with Bildad and Zophar.

That is a problem because, at the end of the book, God tells these men that they have not spoken about Him correctly.

A graph that does that looks authoritative, and it is wrong in the most misleading way possible: it puts the friends’ argument where the narrator’s facts belong.

The issue isn’t that every sentence spoken by the friends must be false. Job 5:13 is a good example of why that conclusion would be too simple. The issue is that the graph needs to preserve the difference between something the text tells us happened and something a character said.

What the graph does instead

In Bible Ontology, statements made by characters carry the identity of the person who said them.

Job 5:13 is not recorded simply as “God catches the wise in their craftiness.” It is recorded as a statement made by Eliphaz. The relationships extracted from that statement retain his name, so someone looking at the graph can see whose words they are.

There are 212 relationships marked as Eliphaz’s words, and another 363 marked as words of Bildad, Zophar, or Elihu. These numbers are counts of extracted relationships, not verse counts like the table above. A single verse can produce more than one relationship.

When the graph cannot identify who is speaking, the statement is held back rather than present it as an unattributed fact. The same principle applies to God’s words. Job 42:7 is recorded as something God says, including His judgment that Job has spoken about Him correctly and the three friends have not.

This isn’t the site deciding which sentences are true and which are false. It is preserving something the book itself makes clear: a speech is something someone said. The reader should be able to see who said it.

So why does Paul quote him?

The text doesn’t explain why Paul chose this particular statement from Job and I think it is worth being careful not to explain more than it does.

God’s words in Job 42:7 tell us that the friends have not spoken about Him correctly. They do not give us a list of every sentence they said that was wrong. And Paul, in 1 Corinthians 3, quotes the line about God catching the wise in their craftiness as part of his argument about the wisdom of the world being foolishness to God. He does not repeat Eliphaz’s argument about Job.

There are a few ways a reader might understand the connection. A statement can be true and still be used in support of a wrong argument. The friends may have understood some things correctly but applied what they knew to Job in a way that was wrong. Or Paul may be making a point by quoting someone whose own argument in Job was ultimately rejected.

Those are possible ways of reading the passages. The text doesn’t explicitly tell us which explanation Paul had in mind.

The Berean editors connect Job 5:13 and 1 Corinthians 3:19 in their notes. Bible Ontology also shows the connection between the two verses. But it does something else as well: it shows that the words in Job 5:13 belong to Eliphaz.

That context matters, even when we don’t settle exactly what Paul intended by quoting him.

I do have my own thoughts about this:

My personal belief is that even foolish speakers say wise things. Quite often the most dangerous argument is a good argument with a misapplication and I do believe that is what was happening in Job. Much of what the three friends argued made logical sense, but they were so sure of themselves that they applied their conclusions where they did not apply. This is why we need to be careful about taking any passage of Scripture out of context, whether that context is who spoke it or what it meant when it was used.

I also personally believe that Paul was making a very subtle point in quoting Eliphaz. He was using Eliphaz as an example of someone who thought he was wise but was foolish in what he was saying. But that is just my opinion, developed from reading the two passages together. The site will never draw that conclusion for you. Its commitment is to link the two passages and provide the appropriate context, then leave it to the reader to sit with the uncertainty and ponder the meaning, as I did.

It isn’t only Job

Job makes this issue especially visible, but it comes up throughout the Bible.

The serpent tells Eve, “You will not surely die” (Genesis 3:4). Job’s wife tells him to “Curse God and die” (Job 2:9). In the opening chapters of Job, we also hear Satan speaking in conversations with God.

All of those words are in the Bible. But the meaning of each statement depends on who said it, who heard it, and what was happening in the passage. Recording the words without preserving that context can give a very different impression of what the text is communicating.

This same question comes up in more difficult places, too. It will come up again when looking at passages about the end times, where visions, speeches, and interpretations sit side by side. It is easy to take a sentence from one of those passages and make it say a great deal. Job is a good place to learn to pay attention to the speaker, because the book itself tells us in its final chapter that the words of some of its most prominent speakers were not right.
