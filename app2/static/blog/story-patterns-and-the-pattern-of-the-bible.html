Story Patterns and the Pattern of the Bible

November 21, 2026

One thing that has intrigued me for years, reading through the Bible, is the sense of déjà vu I get in certain passages. When I read about Jonah asleep in the bottom of the boat while the sea rages and the sailors cry out to their gods, I think of Jesus asleep in the boat while His disciples are sure they are about to drown.

"Jonah had gone down to the lowest part of the vessel, where he lay down and fell into a deep sleep." (Jonah 1:5)

"Suddenly a violent storm came up on the sea, so that the boat was engulfed by the waves. But Jesus was sleeping." (Matthew 8:24)

Those two scenes are now linked in the graph, step by step: the storm, the sleeper, someone waking him, the sea grown calm, the men amazed. It also records where they differ, and the difference is the point: Jonah is thrown into the sea to calm it, and Jesus rebukes the sea Himself. (Jesus calms the storm)

Teaching a graph to notice déjà vu

So one of the capabilities I set out to build into Bible Ontology is pattern discovery. If I could break an event into parts with distinct roles, a beginning, a turn, an outcome, I could compare events across the whole Bible and find the ones that share several of those parts in the same order.

The results surprised me. The mining turned up 16 distinct story patterns and they are laid out in one of my favorite visualizations:

What shapes does the Bible tell its stories in?

Each pattern is a sequence of steps and each story that follows it has each of its steps tied to the verses that tell it. You can open any pattern and read its stories side by side.

The pattern behind the patterns

What the diagram does not show is something larger that, in my view, sits behind several of these patterns. What captivates me most is that the arc of the Bible itself follows them.

My favorite example is Judgment and a remnant spared. Its steps are: wickedness; those to be spared set apart; a judgment that takes the rest; a remnant left; and sometimes a new beginning.

I did not invent this pattern, and neither did the software. Jesus draws it Himself, from two of its stories:

"Just as it was in the days of Noah, so also will it be in the days of the Son of Man ... It was the same in the days of Lot ... But on the day Lot left Sodom, fire and sulfur rained down from heaven and destroyed them all. It will be just like that on the day the Son of Man is revealed." (Luke 17:26–30)

### Two stories side by side

The flood. "Then the LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great upon the earth" (Genesis 6:5). But "Noah, however, found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (6:8). God speaks to Noah, gives him the ark, and Noah does all that God commands. Then: "Go into the ark, you and all your family" (7:1), and "the LORD shut him in" (7:16). The waters take everything else. "But God remembered Noah" (8:1), and a new world begins with a covenant (9:8–11). (The flood)

Sodom. The outcry against Sodom is great (18:20). Before the judgment, Abraham steps forward and speaks to God on the city's behalf: "Will You really sweep away the righteous with the wicked?" (18:23) and he keeps asking, down to ten. The angels take Lot's hand and lead him out (19:16). Fire falls on the cities of the plain. And then the line that, for me, makes the whole story: "So when God destroyed the cities of the plain, He remembered Abraham, and He brought Lot out of the catastrophe" (19:29). (Sodom)

Put them next to each other. In both, judgment comes, and it is real. But in both there is someone who stands between: one who hears from God and acts (Noah builds the ark), one who speaks to God for others (Abraham pleads for the city). And in both, the text says God remembered someone, and a remnant came through.

The ninth story

This example was so compelling to me that I made a rare editorial decision: I added a ninth story to this pattern myself, the cross and the resurrection. (The cross and the resurrection)

I did not make that leap on my own. Jesus made it first, twice. In Luke 17, quoted above, He takes the flood and Sodom and sets His own story beside them: "It will be just like that on the day the Son of Man is revealed." And in John, speaking of His death, He puts the cross at exactly the step where judgment falls:

"Now judgment is upon this world; now the prince of this world will be cast out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw everyone to Myself." He said this to indicate the kind of death He was going to die. (John 12:31–33)

Judgment, and a drawing out of those who will be saved: the same two steps as Noah and Lot, in Jesus' own words about His cross.

Still, it is my reading, and the site says so. Four of the nine stories are marked there as readings rather than as something the text itself states: Korah, the Passover, the fall of Jerusalem, and the cross. Every step of each is still tied to its verses, so you can judge the reading for yourself.

Here is how I read it.

The corruption is all of us. The flood began with "the wickedness of man", and Paul brings it home: "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). It goes back to the beginning: "just as sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin, so also death was passed on to all men, because all sinned" (Romans 5:12). This time the city under judgment is not Sodom. It is everyone.

We have someone who stands between. Noah acted; Abraham spoke to God for others. Paul names the role outright:

"For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5)

The Bible keeps returning to this role. At Sinai the people beg Moses to speak to God for them (Exodus 20:19), and Hebrews calls Jesus the mediator of a better covenant (Hebrews 8:6). The site gathers those passages on one card: A mediator. The Greek word is μεσίτης, mesitēs, literally one who stands in the middle.

The judgment falls, and this time it falls on the mediator. In the flood and in Sodom the one who stood between was spared. At the cross, the mediator takes the judgment Himself: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Matthew 27:46). That is the step where this story breaks the pattern it follows, and to me it is the whole point.

And there is a new beginning. The tomb is empty (Matthew 28:5–7), and "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). After the flood came a new world under a covenant; after the resurrection, a new creation.

What everything points to

Which brings me to the verse most of us learned first:

"For God so loved the world that He gave His one and only Son, that everyone who believes in Him shall not perish but have eternal life." (John 3:16)

John 3:16, drawn in layers: the Greek and the Berean side by side

Noah's family came through the flood. Lot came out of Sodom. In this story, the remnant is not one family. It is "everyone who believes."

If you are a Christian, this is what everything points to. If you are not, I hope the patterns are still worth exploring for yourself. Every step I have described is tied to its verses on the site, and you can check each one.
